Intuitively, it seems that in order to benefit from
their evoluational adaptation of viviparity and to protect
their small yearly batch of fertilized eggs, the pregnant
female surfperch would be required to change their behavior
during the conclusion of their gestation period. The ventral
sides of the pregnant surfperch I observed were obviously
distended and lighter in color than the rest of their body:
at this bloated period they were inevitably less hydrodynamic
than usual. Therefore, it seems appropriate that the
pregnant striped surfperch occurred most densely in the most
protected habitat in my study: the railroad tracks. The
comparatively high numbers of injured pregnant E. lateralis
suggests either that these pregnant perch cannot swim as well
as their lithe counterparts, that the bloated fish are more
attractive to their predators, or both of these hypotheses.
One surprising result was the different distributions of
the two congeners, E. jacksoni and E. lateralis, in
different habitats with approximately the same depth.
