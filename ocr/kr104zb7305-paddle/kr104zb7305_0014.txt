correlates with the Baxter observations of schools of shiner
perch in the study area over the last fifteen years, while
this year it seems that few individuals remain. Nichols also
reported schools of sharpnose surfperch and some appearance
of calico surfperch, neither of which species seem to exist
off of Point Cabrillo this spring. It is interesting that
the shiner and sharpnose perch bear size, shape, and some
dietary similarities to the reef surfperch and
correspondingly, the rainbow and calico perch share size and
pattern characteristics.
Nichols' data showed another interesting decadal
variation that at the same time of year in 1979, larger
densities of black and pile juveniles were observed than
striped juveniles. (Nichols 1979) This comparison is
contradictory to my observations of large numbers of striped
juveniles in 1989. (See figure 9)
Because the number of pregnant striped decreased and the
number of young-of-the-year striped increased as my study
progressed, I believe that I witnessed a critical point: the
end of E. lateralis' five- to six-month gestation period.
